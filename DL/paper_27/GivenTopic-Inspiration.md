Given Topic: Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) constitutes a canonical challenge in multi-agent coordination, where computing collision-free, cost-optimal paths for large teams remains computationally intractable. Contemporary learning-based approaches predominantly depend on pairwise interaction modelling, which fundamentally constrains their capacity to represent simultaneous group dynamics among multiple agents. In densely populated environments, such dyadic paradigms suffer from attentional dispersion, wherein critical coordination signals are suppressed by normalization over numerous irrelevant local entities. Moreover, the representational bottleneck inherent in pairwise message passing conflicts with the combinatorial nature of MAPF, where global optimality generally necessitates reasoning over joint configurations rather than isolated agent dyads. Consequently, prevailing methods demand disproportionately large parameter scales and training regimes to imperfectly approximate these collective dependencies. The central scientific challenge resides in developing principled representational frameworks that natively encode collective multi-agent dependencies to transcend the fundamental limitations of pairwise decomposition in highly-coupled systems.

Given Inspiration:
1. Higher-order relational structures: Multi-agent coordination in dense settings inherently involves simultaneous collective dependencies that resist decomposition into isolated pairwise exchanges, motivating representational frameworks that natively encode multi-way relationships.
2. Attentional dispersion in dense neighborhoods: Localized attention mechanisms that normalize over complete neighborhoods inherently diminish the influence of critical agents when many irrelevant entities are present, suggesting the need for aggregated group-level importance weighting.
3. Joint configuration optimality: The theoretical necessity of reasoning over full joint state spaces for combinatorial optimality in pathfinding implies that effective learning architectures require inductive biases toward collective rather than merely dyadic decision-making.
4. Flexible spatial grouping: Geometric proximity suggests that agents occupying common local regions likely participate in collective interactions, inspiring soft partitioning strategies that avoid rigid spatial boundaries while maintaining computational tractability.
5. Structural priors over scale: The observation that raw model capacity and dataset size alone may be insufficient for complex coordination suggests that injecting domain-appropriate relational priors can achieve superior representational efficiency.
6. Directed asymmetric influence: Multi-agent influence is frequently directional, wherein a subset of agents collectively determines another agent's optimal action, motivating relational structures that distinguish between influencing and influenced parties.
7. Hierarchical signal aggregation: Propagating information from individual entities through intermediate group abstractions before reaching decision targets can better preserve salient interaction signals than flat pairwise aggregation schemes.
