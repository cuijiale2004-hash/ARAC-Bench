Given Topic: The evaluation of video understanding models remains a critical bottleneck in multimodal research. Conventional reference-based metrics, including n-gram overlap and embedding similarity methods, fail to capture semantic fidelity, contextual grounding, and task-specific temporal reasoning inherent in open-ended video understanding tasks. Human evaluation, regarded as the gold standard, is prohibitively expensive, difficult to scale, and subject to significant inter-annotator variability. While large language models have demonstrated efficacy as evaluators in text generation, their extension to video understanding is hindered by the temporal and multimodal complexity of dynamic visual content. The field further lacks large-scale evaluation resources with human preference signals and standardized benchmarks for assessing alignment with human judgments, necessitating reliance on opaque proprietary systems or unreliable zero-shot alternatives. Additionally, current paradigms depend on either vague generic rubrics or manually authored criteria that cannot scale across diverse tasks and domains.

Given Inspiration:
1. The fundamental inadequacy of lexical and shallow semantic similarity metrics for assessing temporally extended, open-ended multimodal outputs that require nuanced perceptual and semantic judgment.
2. The economic and practical constraints of human annotation, which create a severe bottleneck in obtaining large-scale preference signals and reliable ground truth for video understanding evaluation.
3. The brittleness of generic evaluation rubrics and the impracticality of manually authoring scalable, task-specific assessment criteria across diverse video domains and instructions.
4. The self-refinement and self-verification phenomena observed in foundation models, where iterative internal critique and consistency checking can enhance output reliability without external human supervision.
5. The conceptual symmetry between generation and evaluation capabilities, suggesting that a model's understanding of response quality can be harnessed to synthesize structured, graded supervision signals for evaluator training.
6. The recognition that reliable assessment of video understanding requires direct multimodal temporal grounding, as unimodal textual descriptions inadequately capture dynamic visual information for accurate judgment.
7. The principle that smaller evaluators optimized on high-quality, task-specific synthetic supervision can achieve superior alignment with human judgment compared to substantially larger generalist models in zero-shot settings.
8. The necessity of interpretability in automated evaluation systems, motivating the development of evaluators that articulate explicit reasoning and contextualized, instance-specific assessment criteria rather than opaque scalar scores.
