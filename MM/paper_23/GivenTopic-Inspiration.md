Given Topic: In the field of multimodal large language models, the prevailing assumption that capabilities are primarily inherited from the language backbone has led to systematic neglect of the vision encoder’s role in visual perception. Consequently, a critical gap exists in understanding how mainstream post-training optimization paradigms affect the underlying visual representations and perceptual fidelity of such systems, particularly for vision-intensive reasoning tasks. Existing literature lacks a controlled, comprehensive analysis of how distinct alignment strategies during post-training influence the vision encoder’s representational quality and spatial grounding. This study addresses the fundamental question of whether alternative post-training paradigms can fundamentally reshape visual representations to achieve stronger and more localized perception than conventional supervised adaptation. It further seeks to identify efficient methodologies for unlocking the latent potential of existing vision encoders within multimodal architectures, without resorting to prohibitive large-scale pretraining.

Given Inspiration:
1. The transformative impact of reinforcement learning on language model alignment suggests that analogous preference-driven optimization may yield unique benefits for visual perception modules in multimodal architectures.
2. The hypothesis that visual representations within multimodal systems are dynamically reshaped by post-training signals from the language module, rather than remaining frozen or passively inherited from pretraining.
3. The premise that standard instruction-following objectives may optimize for text generation fidelity at the expense of fine-grained visual grounding, necessitating alternative supervisory signals.
4. The suspicion that existing vision encoders are not operating at their full potential when integrated into multimodal frameworks, motivating inquiry into low-cost evolution strategies.
5. The realization that joint optimization with a language head can transmit discriminative signals to early visual representations, potentially refining their semantic selectivity.
6. The suspicion that multimodal performance bottlenecks in visually demanding domains may stem from under-optimized visual representations rather than language-model limitations alone.
7. The concept from cross-modal representation theory that the degree of alignment between visual embeddings and linguistic conceptual spaces is a critical determinant of multimodal reasoning efficacy.
8. The pedagogical analogy that human visual learning is reinforced by evaluative feedback and discriminative comparison, inspiring the investigation of preference-guided signals for artificial vision encoder evolution.
