**Principle 1: Clarification and Empirical Validation of Deterministic Canonicalization versus Probabilistic Invariance in Spanning Tree Cover Construction**

**Definition:**  
In graph canonicalization research, the boundary between deterministic canonicalization and probabilistic invariance is foundational because it determines whether a method yields identical representations for isomorphic graphs or merely invariant distributions. Reviewers expect authors to explicitly characterize the regime in which their method operates: whether it employs a true canonical node labeler that eliminates all symmetry up to isomorphism, or a weaker invariant labeler that requires random tie-breaking and aggregation over multiple samples. The distinction matters critically because weak labelers on highly symmetric or regular graphs may produce unstable representations when only a small number of trees are used, undermining the claim of invariant graph learning. Empirical validation must demonstrate that the finite sample size K is sufficient to stabilize the Monte Carlo approximation of the expected representation across random tie-breaks. Furthermore, the work must analyze adversarial cases such as d-regular graphs where weak labelers assign identical scores to all nodes, and discuss how the framework mitigates these failures through stronger labelers or deterministic alternatives. Terminological precision is also essential; calling a method “canonicalization” when it relies primarily on random sampling invites skepticism unless the probabilistic mechanism is rigorously justified as a controlled, canonicalization-driven interpolation. Overall, this principle ensures that invariance claims are neither overstated nor obfuscated by imprecise language.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Invariance Regime Characterization:** Does the work explicitly define whether invariance is deterministic or probabilistic, and map the choice of node labeler onto this spectrum?
- **Finite-Sample Stability:** Is there empirical evidence (e.g., variance across runs, coverage saturation curves) that the chosen number of trees K yields stable representations without requiring exponentially many samples?
- **Adversarial Symmetry Analysis:** Does the work identify failure modes on highly symmetric graphs and provide remedies such as canonical labelers or centrality-based tie-breaking?
- **Terminological Rigor:** Is the term “canonicalization” used in a manner consistent with the actual invariance guarantee, distinguishing it from unstructured sampling schemes?


**Principle 2: Bridging Theoretical Distortion Guarantees for Reference Distributions with Empirical Behavior of Coverage-Aware Tree Covers**

**Definition:**  
When theoretical guarantees are established for idealized reference distributions—such as uniform spanning trees for distortion bounds or worst-case arboricity conditions for coverage—the burden of proof shifts to demonstrating that the practical algorithm inherits these desiderata. In the evaluation of tree-cover canonicalization, reviewers scrutinize whether theorems proved for stylized settings actually describe the empirical behavior of the implemented coverage-aware minimum spanning tree sampler. The gap between sufficient theoretical conditions and practical hyperparameters must be quantified rather than assumed; for instance, a lemma may require a penalty parameter τ above a problem-dependent threshold, while experiments use a fixed small value. High-quality research provides empirical verification that the practical tree distribution achieves full edge coverage, low stretch, and low contraction even when theoretical sufficient conditions are not strictly met. Additionally, the work should directly compare the empirical distortion of the actual algorithm against the reference distribution to show that the engineered distribution meets or exceeds the theoretical baseline. This alignment between theory and practice prevents the method from resting on inapplicable formal guarantees and builds reviewer confidence in its structural properties. Ultimately, the principle demands that distance-preservation claims be grounded in measurements of the algorithm as deployed, not only as analyzed under idealized assumptions.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Reference-to-Actual Verification:** Does the work empirically measure distortion and coverage for the implemented tree distribution and compare it to the theoretical reference (e.g., UST)?
- **Sufficient vs. Necessary Conditions:** Are practical hyperparameters (e.g., τ = 1) validated empirically for coverage and distortion, with acknowledgment that theoretical bounds may be sufficient but not necessary?
- **Quantitative Distortion Metrics:** Are both stretch and contraction reported for the actual method across diverse graph families, not just asymptotic bounds?
- **Coverage Empiricism:** Is there direct measurement of edge coverage as a function of the number of trees K on real datasets, confirming that logarithmic-size covers are achieved in practice?


**Principle 3: Demonstration of Strict Expressivity Beyond the Node Labeler and 1-WL via Isomorphism Stress Tests and Strong Baselines**

**Definition:**  
A central motivation for moving beyond message-passing neural networks is to escape the expressivity limitations of 1-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman tests, but such claims require rigorous, targeted validation rather than indirect inference from downstream accuracy. Reviewers demand that works proposing expressive graph representations include synthetic isomorphism-testing benchmarks—such as CSL or EXP datasets—specifically constructed to expose the failures of 1-WL and 2-WL. Furthermore, fair evaluation necessitates comparison against state-of-the-art subgraph GNNs and structural encoding methods that are provably more expressive than 1-WL, rather than comparing solely against standard MPNNs or sequence-based canonicalizations. A critical nuance is whether the method is bottlenecked by its initial node labeler; if the labeler itself is only as powerful as 1-WL, the downstream architecture must empirically demonstrate that it can still distinguish graphs that the labeler cannot. This requires showing that even with inexpensive labelers like degree-based initialization, the method surpasses the labeler’s own distinguishability on challenging graph families. Conceptual clarity is also required to differentiate the approach from superficially similar hierarchical graph-to-tree methods, explaining why the proposed decomposition preserves distances and invariance in ways that prior tree-based architectures do not. Without such benchmarks and comparisons, expressivity claims remain speculative and may be dismissed as incremental. This principle therefore mandates both synthetic stress tests and head-to-head evaluation with the strongest available expressive competitors.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Synthetic Isomorphism Benchmarks:** Does the work evaluate performance on datasets like CSL where 1-WL and 2-WL fail, providing direct evidence of superior distinguishability?
- **High-Expressivity Baselines:** Are comparisons made against subgraph GNNs, structural encodings, or k-WL methods rather than only standard MPNNs or canonical sequences?
- **Labeler Bottleneck Analysis:** Is there empirical proof that the method exceeds the expressivity of its initial node labeler, for example by outperforming 1-WL/2-WL methods using only a degree-based labeler?
- **Conceptual Differentiation:** Does the work clearly distinguish its decomposition from prior graph-to-tree hierarchies, articulating unique structural or invariance properties?


**Principle 4: Ablation and Geometric Justification of Canonical Structure Control Against Unstructured Random Tree Decompositions**

**Definition:**  
Because recurrent tree encoders are inherently sensitive to hierarchy and root placement, the quality of a tree-cover representation depends heavily on whether the decomposition is structurally controlled or arbitrarily random. Reviewers expect authors to justify their canonical construction—covering root selection, edge weighting strategy, and tree shape—through direct ablations that replace canonical components with random alternatives. Such ablations isolate whether performance gains stem from the tree cover itself or from the specific canonicalization that guides it, distinguishing principled geometric preservation from unstructured subgraph sampling. The geometric rationale should explain how choices such as tree-center rooting and coverage-aware edge penalties respect the underlying graph metric, preventing unbalanced trees that force information propagation over unnecessarily long paths. Additionally, the role of residual non-tree edges must be analyzed to confirm that local connectivity missed by individual spanning trees is adequately recovered without reintroducing the pathologies of full message passing. Performance variance under different canonical configurations should also be reported to establish robustness. Without this structural analysis, the method risks being viewed as a generic ensemble of random trees rather than a carefully designed canonical representation. This principle ensures that tree-based decompositions are validated as geometry-aware constructions, not merely convenient data augmentations.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Random Decomposition Ablation:** Does the work include ablations replacing canonical tree covers with random spanning trees and random roots to quantify the benefit of structured decomposition?
- **Geometric Rationale for Rooting:** Is there explicit justification for root selection (e.g., tree centers) that minimizes path lengths and respects graph symmetry?
- **Residual Edge Analysis:** Are ablations included for handling non-tree edges, such as removing the residual MPNN or replacing the tree encoder, to isolate their contributions?
- **Structural Robustness:** Is performance variance reported across different canonical tree shapes or coverage penalties, confirming that gains are systematic rather than artifacts of a specific random seed?


**Principle 5: Scalability Assessment and Domain Generality Beyond Sparse Biochemical Graphs for Canonicalization Pipelines**

**Definition:**  
Theoretical guarantees for tree-cover methods often assume bounded arboricity and logarithmic covers that are most favorable on sparse graphs, raising questions about whether the approach remains practical and beneficial on larger, denser, or non-biochemical datasets. Reviewers require quantitative evidence of preprocessing runtime and memory overhead, especially since canonicalization must be computed once per dataset and stored throughout training. It is insufficient to assert efficiency; the work must report per-graph preprocessing times and storage costs across a spectrum of graph sizes and densities, including regimes where theoretical sparse-graph guarantees weaken. Moreover, empirical evaluation should extend beyond molecular and protein benchmarks—domains where canonicalization is already prevalent—to larger networks such as brain connectomes, social networks, or knowledge graphs that test the method’s generality. The work should also honestly discuss limitations in dense regimes, acknowledging where distortion bounds degrade or tree cover size requirements grow. Comparing preprocessing overhead against alternatives like per-epoch random walk sampling helps justify the canonicalization paradigm. This principle ensures that tree-cover methods are evaluated as scalable, general-purpose graph learning frameworks rather than narrow improvements on small, sparse benchmarks.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Quantitative Efficiency Metrics:** Are preprocessing runtime and memory overhead (e.g., O(Kn) storage) reported for datasets of varying size and density?
- **Beyond Sparse Biochemical Domains:** Does the evaluation include larger or denser graphs outside standard molecular/protein benchmarks, such as brain networks or social graphs?
- **Dense-Graph Limitations:** Does the work acknowledge where theoretical guarantees weaken for dense graphs and discuss empirical behavior in these regimes?
- **Cost-Benefit Justification:** Is the preprocessing cost contextualized relative to alternatives like sampling-based methods, demonstrating that the one-time overhead is justified by training-time savings or accuracy gains?
