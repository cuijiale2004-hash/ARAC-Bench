# 1. Research Background and Existing Pain Points

Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms are highly sensitive to reward function specification, which remains a central challenge limiting their broad applicability. A central challenge in reinforcement learning (RL) is the design of effective reward functions for complex tasks. The shape of the reward influences the complexity of the problem at hand; for instance, sparse rewards provide an insufficient learning signal, making it difficult for agents to improve. Even hand-crafted dense rewards are susceptible to unintended loopholes or "reward hacking", where an agent exploits the specification without achieving the true objective. The unifying challenge is thus how to communicate complex objectives to an agent in a manner that provides structured, actionable guidance.

While Foundation Models (FMs) excel at interpreting and decomposing tasks from natural language, a critical gap exists in translating this abstract understanding into the concrete structured reward signals necessary for RL. Consequently, high-level plans generated by FMs often fail to ground effectively, leaving the agent without the granular feedback required for learning. To bridge this gap, Reward Machines (RMs), an automata-based formalism, are utilized. By decomposing tasks into a finite automaton of sub-goals, RMs provide a compositional structure for both rewards and policies that is inherently more structured and verifiable than monolithic reward functions. While theoretically principled, their practical application has been confined to task-specific applications due to the complexity of their manual, expert-driven design. The existing pain points are the difficulty of communicating complex objectives via sparse rewards, the vulnerability of dense rewards to hacking, the grounding gap between FMs and RL, and the manual expert-driven complexity of designing Reward Machines.

# 2. Core Research Motivation and Scientific Questions

The core research motivation is to bridge the critical gap between the semantic reasoning of foundation models and the low-level control of reinforcement learning agents. The scientific questions addressed are: How to communicate complex objectives to an agent in a manner that provides structured, actionable guidance? How to automate the design and construction of Reward Machines, thereby unlocking their potential to solve the broader challenge of communicating complex objectives in RL? How to translate abstract human intent (natural language) into a concrete learning signal for solving complex tasks using the reasoning and code-generation capabilities of FMs? The authors posit that the reasoning and code-generation capabilities of modern FMs are well-suited to automate the design and construction of RMs, thereby unlocking their potential to solve the broader challenge of communicating complex objectives in RL; the resulting RMs can thus translate abstract human intent into a concrete learning signal for solving complex tasks.

# 3. Overall Core Idea and Design Philosophy

The overall core idea is the presentation of ARM-FM: Automated Reward Machines via Foundation Models, a framework for automated, compositional reward design in RL that leverages the high-level reasoning capabilities of foundation models (FMs). Reward machines (RMs) – an automata-based formalism for reward specification – are used as the mechanism for RL objective specification, and are automatically constructed via the use of FMs. The structured formalism of RMs yields effective task decompositions, while the use of FMs enables objective specifications in natural language. The design philosophy bridges the gap between FM semantic reasoning and low-level RL control by automatically generating Language-Aligned Reward Machines (LARMs) from natural language. By conditioning a single policy on the embeddings of the LARM’s natural language state descriptions, the reward machine is transformed from a static plan into a compositional library of reusable skills.

# 4. Core Innovation Points

1. A novel framework for automatically generating complete task specifications directly from natural language using foundation models.
2. The introduction of language-aligned reward machines (LARMs) which include the automaton structure, executable labeling functions, and natural language instructions for each subtask.
3. A method that leverages the language-aligned nature of the resulting automata to create a shared skill space, enabling effective experience reuse and policy transfer across related tasks.
4. Extensive empirical validation demonstrating that the framework solves complex, long-horizon tasks across multiple domains that are generally intractable for standard RL methods.
5. The framework dramatically improves sample-efficiency by converting sparse rewards into dense, structured learning signals.
6. The framework enables efficient multi-task training and zero-shot generalization.

# 5. Overview of the Overall Technical Solution

The ARM-FM framework comprises two major components: (i) the introduction of Language-Aligned RMs (LARMs), which are automatically constructed using FMs; and (ii) their integration into RL training by conditioning policies on language embeddings of RM states, enabling structured rewards, generalization, and skill reuse. Given a high-level natural language prompt and a visual observation of the environment, a FM automatically generates the formal specification of the Reward Machine, the executable Python code for the labeling functions, and the natural language descriptions for each RM state. During the RL training loop, the labeling functions evaluate environment observations to update the Reward Machine’s state, which provides a dense reward signal. The RL agent’s policy receives the environment observation along with the embedding of the current RM state’s language description, making it aware of its active sub-goal. The framework employs N rounds of self-improvement using paired generator and critic FMs to progressively refine the RM specification. The compositional structure of LARMs enables zero-shot generalization to novel tasks composed of previously seen sub-goals.

# 6. Detailed Module Design

**MDP Formalism Module:**
We assume the standard RL formalism, which defines an environment as a Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) $\langle S,A,R,P\rangle$, where $S$ is the set of MDP states, $A$ is the set of possible actions, $R : S \times A \rightarrow R$ is the MDP reward function, and $P : S \times A \rightarrow \Delta(S)$ is a probabilistic MDP transition function.

**Reward Machine Module:**
A Reward Machine (RM) is a finite-state automaton that encodes complex, temporally extended, and potentially non-Markovian RL tasks. We formally define an RM by the tuple $\langle U, u_I ,\Sigma, \delta, R, F,L\rangle$. Each RM state $u \in U$ can be thought of as representing a subtask, and the transitions $u' = \delta(u, \sigma)$ denote progress to a new stage of the overall objective after a particular event $\sigma \in \Sigma$ occurs in the environment. The RM reward function $R(u, s, a, s')$ assigns a reward based on the current RM state $u$ and the underlying MDP transition $(s, a, s')$. Meanwhile, the set of final RM states $F$ defines the conditions under which the task described by the RM is complete. Finally, the labeling function $L$ is required to connect the RM’s events, transitions, and rewards to states and actions from the underlying MDP.

**Language-Aligned Reward Machines (LARMs) Module:**
We define LARMs as RMs that are additionally equipped with natural-language instructions $l_u$ for each RM state $u$, and with an embedding function $\phi(\cdot)$ that maps such language instructions to an embedding vector $z_u = \phi(l_u) \in R^d$. By equipping RM states with embedding vectors $z_u$ that encode language-based descriptions of the corresponding subtasks, we provide the first mechanism for constructing a semantically grounded skill space in RMs: policies conditioned on these embeddings can naturally share knowledge across related subtasks, enabling transfer, compositionality, and zero-shot generalization.

**LARM Generation Module:**
We present a framework to automatically construct LARMs from language-and-image-based task descriptions by iteratively prompting an FM. To progressively refine the RM specification, we employ N rounds of self-improvement using paired generator and critic FMs. A human may optionally intervene by approving the output or providing corrective feedback.

**RL Integration Module:**
The introduction of the LARM uses an augmented state space that is the cross-product of the MDP and RM states $(S \times U)$, and a reward function that is the sum of the MDP and RM rewards; we will refer to this augmented MDP as $\mathcal{M}'$. At timestep $t$, the agent selects actions conditioned on the environment state and the language embedding of the current LARM state: $\pi(s_t, z_{u_t})$. This language-based policy conditioning is the central mechanism enabling generalization in our framework, creating a semantically grounded skill space where instructions like “pick up a blue key" and "pick up a red key" are naturally close in the embedding space, unlocking a pathway for broad experience reuse and efficient policy transfer. During training, after the agent executes an action $a_t \sim \pi(s_t, z_{u_t})$, the underlying MDP transitions to $s_{t+1}$ and returns a reward $R_t$. The labeling function $L(s_{t+1}, a_t)$ determines if a symbolic event has occurred, which may induce a LARM transition $u_{t+1} = \delta(u_t,L(s_{t+1}, a_t))$, as well as an additional reward $R^{RM}_t$. The sum of the MDP and RM rewards are then used for learning: $R^{total}_t = R_t + R^{RM}_t$.

**Generalization Module:**
The compositional structure of LARMs enables zero-shot generalization to novel tasks composed of previously seen sub-goals. We design an experiment where $\pi$ is trained on a set of tasks, $\{T_A, T_B\}$, each with an associated LARM, $R_A$ and $R_B$. During training, the policy learns skills corresponding to the union of all sub-goal embeddings, $\{z_u|u \in U_A \cup U_B\}$. At evaluation time, we introduce a new, unseen task, $T_C$, with a novel LARM, $R_C$, generated by the FM. Zero-shot success is possible if the set of sub-goals in the new task is composed of elements semantically familiar from training, i.e., if for any state $u' \in U_C$, its embedding $z_{u'}$ is close to an embeddings seen during training. The policy receives the input $(s_t, z_{u'_t})$. Because the embedding $z_{u'_t}$ is already located in a familiar region of the skill space, the policy can reuse the relevant learned behavior to make progress and solve the unseen composite task.

**Theoretical Properties Module:**
[Optimality Preservation] Assume the generated LARM $\mathcal{A}$ contains no positive reward cycles (i.e., for any cycle $u_i \rightarrow ... \rightarrow u_i$, the sum of rewards $R_{LARM}$ along the cycle is $\le 0$). Assume also that the final reward $R_{goal}$ (obtained on transition to an accepting state $u \in F$) is strictly greater than the cumulative reward of any non-terminal trajectory. Then, a policy $\pi^*$ that is optimal for the cross-product MDP $\mathcal{M}_{LARM}$ is also optimal for the original sparse MDP $\mathcal{M}$.
Proof Sketch. The condition of no positive reward cycles is key. It ensures that the value of any non-terminal looping trajectory is bounded and not preferable to progressing toward the goal. Any cycles in the LARM (e.g., for losing progress) must have a non-positive cumulative reward, which prevents the agent from creating reward traps. Because the terminal reward $R_{goal}$ is set to be strictly dominant, the optimal policy for $\mathcal{M}_{LARM}$ will always maximize value by finding a path to an accepting state $u \in F$. The intermediate rewards from $R_{LARM}$ thus act as potential-based shaping to guide exploration, densifying the sparse signal without altering the set of optimal policies.

# 7. All Mathematical Formulas and Symbol Definitions

MDP definition: $\langle S,A,R,P\rangle$
$S$: set of MDP states
$A$: set of possible actions
$R : S \times A \rightarrow R$: MDP reward function
$P : S \times A \rightarrow \Delta(S)$: probabilistic MDP transition function

RM definition: $\langle U, u_I ,\Sigma, \delta, R, F,L\rangle$
$U$: finite set of RM states
$u_I$: initial state of the RM
$\Sigma$: finite set of symbols representing events that cause transitions in the RM
$\delta : U \times \Sigma \rightarrow U$: deterministic RM transition function
$R : U \times S \times A \times S \rightarrow R$: RM reward function
$F \subseteq U$: set of final RM states
$L : S \times A \rightarrow \Sigma$: labeling function that connects MDP states $s \in S$ and actions $a \in A$ to the RM event symbols $\sigma \in \Sigma$
$u' = \delta(u, \sigma)$: RM transition denoting progress to a new stage after event $\sigma$
$R(u, s, a, s')$: RM reward function assigns a reward based on current RM state $u$ and underlying MDP transition $(s, a, s')$

LARM definitions:
$l_u$: natural-language instructions for each RM state $u$
$\phi(\cdot)$: embedding function that maps language instructions to an embedding vector
$z_u = \phi(l_u) \in R^d$: embedding vector for RM state $u$

RL Integration definitions:
$S \times U$: augmented state space that is the cross-product of the MDP and RM states
$\mathcal{M}'$: augmented MDP
$\pi(s_t, z_{u_t})$: policy conditioned on the environment state and the language embedding of the current LARM state
$a_t \sim \pi(s_t, z_{u_t})$: action selection
$s_{t+1}$: next MDP state
$R_t$: MDP reward
$L(s_{t+1}, a_t)$: labeling function evaluation
$u_{t+1} = \delta(u_t,L(s_{t+1}, a_t))$: LARM transition
$R^{RM}_t$: additional reward from LARM transition
$R^{total}_t = R_t + R^{RM}_t$: total reward used for learning

Generalization definitions:
$\{T_A, T_B\}$: set of training tasks
$R_A, R_B$: associated LARMs
$\{z_u|u \in U_A \cup U_B\}$: union of all sub-goal embeddings
$T_C$: new, unseen task
$R_C$: novel LARM for Task C
$u' \in U_C$: state in Task C RM
$z_{u'}$: embedding for state $u'$
$\pi(s_t, z_{u'_t})$: policy input for zero-shot generalization

Theoretical Properties definitions:
$\mathcal{A}$: generated LARM $\langle U, u_0, F, \delta, R_{LARM}\rangle$
$u_i \rightarrow ... \rightarrow u_i$: cycle in RM
$R_{goal}$: final reward obtained on transition to accepting state $u \in F$
$\mathcal{M}_{LARM}$: cross-product MDP
$\mathcal{M}$: original sparse MDP
$\pi^*$: optimal policy

# 8. Algorithm Pseudocode

Algorithm 1 DQN Training with Language-Aligned Reward Machines (LARMs)
1: Initialize: Replay memory D to capacity N .
2: Initialize: Q-network Q with random weights $\theta$.
3: Initialize: Target Q-network $\hat{Q}$ with weights $\theta^- \leftarrow \theta$.
4: Initialize: Update frequency C for the target network.
5: Input: LARM $(U, u_I , \delta, R,L)$ from Section 2.1.
6: Input: State instruction embedding function $\phi(\cdot)$.
7: for episode = 1 to M do
8: Reset environment to get initial state $s_0$.
9: Reset LARM to its initial state, $u_0 \leftarrow u_I$.
10: for t = 0 to T-1 do
11: With probability $\epsilon$, select a random action $a_t$.
12: Otherwise, select $a_t = \arg\max_a Q(s_t, \phi(u_t), a; \theta)$.
13: Execute action $a_t$ in the environment, observe reward $R_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$.
14: Get LARM event via labeling function: $e_t = L(s_{t+1}, a_t)$.
15: Get next LARM state: $u_{t+1} = \delta(u_t, e_t)$.
16: Get LARM reward: $R^{RM}_t = R(u_t, e_t)$.
17: Compute total reward: $R^{total}_t = R_t + R^{RM}_t$.
18: Store transition $(s_t, u_t, a_t, R^{total}_t, s_{t+1}, u_{t+1})$ in D.
19: Sample a random minibatch of transitions $(s_j , u_j , a_j , R^{total}_j , s_{j+1}, u_{j+1})$ from D.
20: Set target $y_j =$
$\begin{cases} R^{total}_j & \text{if episode terminates at step } j + 1 \\ R^{total}_j + \gamma \max_{a'} \hat{Q}(s_{j+1}, \phi(u_{j+1}), a'; \theta^-) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
21: Perform a gradient descent step on $(y_j - Q(s_j , \phi(u_j), a_j ; \theta))^2$.
22: Every C steps, update the target network: $\theta^- \leftarrow \theta$.
23: end for
24: end for
