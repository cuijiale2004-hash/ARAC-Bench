Given Topic: The research addresses continual adaptation in generative recommender systems built upon large language models, where user-item interactions arrive chronologically and user preferences exhibit temporal drift. Mainstream parameter-efficient continual learning strategies predominantly prioritize exhaustive retention of previously acquired task knowledge. Yet in recommendation, historical preferences frequently become obsolete; their rigid preservation can impede prediction accuracy when user interests undergo substantial shifts, whereas straightforward sequential fine-tuning induces catastrophic forgetting of enduring behavioral patterns. The fundamental challenge resides in reconciling stability and plasticity: selectively maintaining historically predictive signals that retain future relevance while accommodating evolving user interests, without relying on task-disjoint assumptions or the mechanical accumulation of past model components.

Given Inspiration:
1. The mismatch between conventional continual learning formulations, which assume task disjointness and time-agnostic interference, and recommendation dynamics characterized by recurring users and continuously evolving preferences.
2. The recognition that historical knowledge in recommendation contains both persistent long-term tendencies and transient outdated signals, necessitating selective retention rather than uniform preservation.
3. Proximal regularization principles in optimization, where anchoring updates to an immediate prior state provides controlled flexibility superior to aggregating all historical states.
4. Data-aware directional interpolation, wherein the magnitude of adaptation along each parameter dimension should scale with the statistical support offered by incoming data.
5. Structural heterogeneity across functional modules, implying that distinct parameter groups warrant differentiated regularization intensities rather than homogeneous penalties.
6. The stability-plasticity dilemma in dynamical adaptation, wherein effective systems must resist deformation along established predictive axes while remaining pliable to emerging distributional shifts.
