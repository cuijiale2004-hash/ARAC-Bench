**Principle 1: Conceptual and Empirical Distinction of Dynamic Adversarial Critics from Static Generative Verifiers**

**Definition:**  
In free-form generation tasks where outputs must satisfy diverse, implicit rubrics, reviewers must assess whether a proposed adversarial critic framework represents a genuine methodological advance over static generative verifiers and fixed reward models. The core issue is whether the critic operates as an adaptive, jointly trained policy within a closed-loop min-max optimization, or merely as a static evaluator that outputs fixed judgments regardless of generator progress. A dynamic critic should demonstrably evolve its probing strategy as the generator improves, shifting from coarse initial heuristics to targeted, high-value failure modes. This distinction is crucial because static evaluators are functionally equivalent to reward models that remain vulnerable to reward hacking and distribution shift. Furthermore, the work must provide mechanistic insight into how the adversarial game shapes an implicit curriculum of increasing difficulty. Without such evidence, the contribution risks being perceived as incremental repackaging of existing verifier-based approaches.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Closed-Loop Adaptation**: Does the work empirically demonstrate that the critic policy meaningfully evolves during training (e.g., via qualitative case studies or quantitative detection-rate curves), rather than converging to static or degenerate patterns?
- **Functional Differentiation**: Is there a crisp conceptual separation from generative verifiers? Specifically, does the critic propose verifiable rubrics for external validation rather than directly assigning scalar rewards or explanations, thereby eliminating reward-hacking surfaces?
- **Mechanistic Insight**: Does the analysis explain how the adversarial game shapes a curriculum of increasing difficulty, and does it avoid conflating final task performance with genuine critic-generator co-adaptation?

**Principle 2: Circularity-Resistant Validation Protocol and Cross-Family Robustness Verification**

**Definition:**  
When external validators or reference solutions supervise training—particularly in code generation—reviewers must scrutinize whether the validation pipeline introduces data leakage, same-family memorization, or circular reasoning. A rigorous protocol must ensure that neither the generator nor the critic observes reference solutions, execution traces, or derived information that could shortcut learning. Reported improvements must stem from the adversarial learning procedure itself rather than model-family-specific priors or exploitation of validator artifacts. This principle demands cross-family control experiments where reference solutions are generated by a fundamentally different model family to rule out backbone-specific leakage. It also requires that the validator function as a true black box, exposing only a binary correctness flag without semantic gradients or intermediate outputs. Adherence to these standards separates genuine generalization from circular self-supervision.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Information Isolation**: Are the generator and critic strictly prevented from accessing reference solutions, intermediate traces, or execution outputs that could leak correct answers?
- **Cross-Family Validation**: Does the work include control experiments using reference solutions generated by a different model family (e.g., GPT-4o for a Qwen generator) to rule out same-backbone leakage, and do results remain consistent?
- **Validator Black-Box Integrity**: Is the validator designed as a true black-box function that only returns a binary correctness flag, with no gradient or semantic information flowing back to the policies?

**Principle 3: Verification Efficiency and Reward Signal Fidelity in Sparse Binary Validation Schemes**

**Definition:**  
For tasks with expensive or non-enumerable verification, reviewers must evaluate whether the proposed reward scheme achieves an optimal balance between verification cost and learning signal quality. The central tension lies in exhaustive per-rubric verification, which is accurate but computationally intractable, versus sparse binary rewards, which are scalable but potentially information-poor. High-quality research must justify why a sparse binary signal suffices for effective preference optimization, typically by showing that denser multi-level rewards amplify noise from retrieval randomness or overlapping rubrics. The work should demonstrate empirical scaling advantages as output complexity increases, such as linearly growing verification gaps between exhaustive and dynamic methods. Additionally, it must quantify the early-training prevalence of informative preference pairs to confirm that the binary signal is sufficient for algorithms like DPO. Without this justification, claims of efficiency may mask degraded learning dynamics.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Scalability Evidence**: Does the work show that verification costs grow sub-linearly (or are dramatically reduced) relative to exhaustive baselines as task complexity increases (e.g., from 4-sentence to 8-sentence generation), without sacrificing final accuracy?
- **Sparse-Reward Justification**: Is there a principled argument—supported by ablation or empirical comparison—explaining why dense/multi-level rewards perform worse than binary validation (e.g., due to retrieval noise or overlapping rubrics creating unstable intermediate scores)?
- **Informative Preference Pair Rate**: Does the work quantify the proportion of generated candidate sets that yield non-degenerate 0-1 preference pairs, especially early in training, to confirm that the sparse signal is sufficient for DPO or similar objectives?

**Principle 4: Empirical Rigor via Statistical Uncertainty Quantification and Cross-Domain Backbone Robustness**

**Definition:**  
Because adversarial training can exhibit high variance and sensitivity to initialization, reviewers demand rigorous statistical characterization and broad generalization tests beyond a single domain or backbone. This includes multi-seed replication with reported mean and variance to establish that observed improvements are statistically significant rather than artifacts of randomness. Evaluations should span multiple model families to ensure conclusions are not backbone-specific, as training dynamics may vary with different pre-trained priors. Furthermore, testing on diverse domains—such as medical question answering in addition to Wikipedia biographies—demonstrates that the method generalizes across distinct semantic structures and rubric spaces. This principle ensures that the approach represents a robust scientific contribution rather than an overfit engineering pipeline. Ultimately, cross-domain and cross-backbone validation provide the necessary evidence for community confidence in the method's reliability.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Statistical Replication**: Are results reported with mean and standard deviation across multiple random seeds, and do confidence intervals clearly separate the method from baselines?
- **Backbone Independence**: Does the evaluation include at least one alternative backbone (e.g., LLaMA instead of Qwen) to verify that training dynamics and efficiency gains are not unique to a specific model family?
- **Cross-Domain Generalization**: Are experiments conducted on additional domains (e.g., MedicalQA beyond Wikipedia biographies) with distinct semantic structures and rubric spaces to demonstrate broad applicability?

**Principle 5: Methodological Transparency and Hyperparameter Sensitivity in Generator-Critic Min-Max Training**

**Definition:**  
The practical instantiation of a min-max game between generator and critic involves critical hyperparameters that control exploration, verification budget, and preference pair availability. Reviewers must assess whether the work provides clear, task-specific rationale for choices such as the number of candidate outputs and critic proposals, rather than treating them as opaque constants. These parameters govern a fundamental trade-off: larger critic proposal sets expand failure-mode exploration but reduce the number of passing candidates available for DPO, while also inflating verification costs. A transparent analysis should explain why configurations differ across tasks—such as factual text versus code generation—based on properties like rubric diversity, dataset scale, or test determinism. Without such methodological transparency, the training procedure risks appearing as an irreproducible engineering hack rather than a principled algorithmic contribution. Consequently, hyperparameter sensitivity and task-adaptive design constitute essential evaluation dimensions.

**Core Evaluation Criteria:**
- **Hyperparameter Rationale**: Are the choices of key parameters (e.g., candidate count and critic proposal count) explicitly justified in terms of prior work standards, preference-pair sufficiency for DPO, and the structure of the rubric/test space?
- **Trade-off Analysis**: Does the work analyze the tension between large proposal sets (which expand failure-mode exploration but reduce passing candidates and increase cost) and conservative settings (which may miss critical errors)?
- **Task-Specific Adaptation**: Does the paper explain why hyperparameters differ across tasks based on domain properties such as rubric diversity, dataset size, or test determinism, rather than presenting a one-size-fits-all configuration?
