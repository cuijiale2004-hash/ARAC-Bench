Given Topic: Research in autonomous long-horizon agents seeks to build systems capable of extended reasoning and acting in complex, dynamic environments. Current training paradigms face a fundamental dilemma: imitation learning on expert trajectories yields efficient but brittle policies that fail to generalize to novel situations, while reinforcement learning with sparse outcome rewards encourages exploration yet inadvertently reinforces flawed reasoning paths. The latter phenomenon, characterized by redundant actions and illogical loops despite eventual task success, indicates that agents optimize for terminal outcomes without acquiring coherent problem-solving processes. Consequently, agents exploit spurious correlations rather than developing robust, transferable reasoning. The core scientific issue is how to provide dense, process-level supervision that shapes intermediate cognitive behaviors during end-to-end training, thereby bridging the gap between task completion and reasoning quality to achieve both generalization and efficiency.

Given Inspiration:
1. Metacognitive theory from cognitive science, which posits that effective problem-solving depends on self-awareness and active regulation of one's own cognitive processes, rather than purely on domain knowledge.
2. The critical observation that optimizing solely for terminal task success in long-horizon agents leads to a decoupling between outcome achievement and reasoning quality, resulting in brittle or inefficient behavior.
3. The perspective that high-level cognitive functions—including strategic planning, hypothesis-driven exploration, execution monitoring, and error reflection—constitute distinct, identifiable primitives that can be isolated for targeted supervision.
4. The insight that learning objectives should incorporate both global task-completion signals and local process-fidelity signals to distinguish robust reasoning from spuriously successful trajectories.
5. The hypothesis that rewarding beneficial intermediate cognitive behaviors, rather than only final outcomes, can align the agent's internal decision-making structure with generalizable problem-solving principles.
