Given Topic: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models on complex tasks. However, prevailing approaches predominantly rely on on-policy optimization, wherein experiences generated during rollout phases are discarded after a single gradient update. This practice incurs substantial computational inefficiency, forfeits opportunities to learn from prior successful explorations, and imposes a bottleneck on scaling reinforcement learning for large reasoning models. Furthermore, while experience replay is widely adopted in general reinforcement learning, its application to reasoning models remains underexplored, and the characteristics that determine the value of reasoning experiences have not been systematically investigated. The core scientific issue is to identify what constitutes valuable reasoning experience and to develop principled strategies for organizing, selecting, and replaying past experiences to improve sample efficiency and training stability without compromising exploratory diversity.

Given Inspiration:
1. Classical experience replay and prioritized learning in reinforcement learning, which establish that historical interactions contain substantial latent value for stabilizing training and enhancing sample efficiency beyond single-use on-policy data.
2. The empirical observation that reasoning experience quality correlates with measurable trajectory properties—specifically, intermediate task difficulty and reduced generation uncertainty—suggesting that not all successful rollouts are equally instructive.
3. The phenomenon that trajectories exhibiting high internal uncertainty, even when yielding correct final answers, often reflect flawed underlying reasoning chains; replaying such fortuitous successes risks entrenching systematic errors through a compounding snowball effect.
4. Curriculum learning principles indicating that learning signals are maximized when models focus on partially mastered problems rather than trivially easy or intractably hard ones, necessitating dynamic difficulty-aware organization of experiential data.
5. The exploration-exploitation dilemma in policy optimization, where aggressive reuse of past experiences can suppress policy diversity, suggesting that off-policy replay must be carefully balanced with fresh exploration to preserve robust reasoning capabilities.
