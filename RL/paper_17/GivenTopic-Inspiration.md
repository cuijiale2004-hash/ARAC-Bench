Given Topic: Reinforcement learning systems remain critically dependent on reward function specification, which constitutes a major barrier to broad applicability. Existing approaches face a fundamental tension: sparse reward signals provide insufficient guidance for learning in complex environments, while manually engineered dense rewards are susceptible to exploitation and require substantial domain expertise. Furthermore, although contemporary foundation models demonstrate strong capability in interpreting high-level task descriptions from natural language, translating this abstract semantic understanding into concrete, structured reward signals suitable for low-level control remains largely unresolved. Current structured formalisms capable of representing temporally extended objectives offer compositional and verifiable properties, yet their practical deployment is severely constrained by reliance on manual, expert-driven construction. The core problem addressed is how to automatically derive structured, interpretable, and compositional reward specifications directly from high-level natural language intent, thereby providing actionable incremental guidance for agents in long-horizon, sparse-reward settings while facilitating cross-task skill transfer and generalization.

Given Inspiration:
1. Automata-theoretic models of task specification offer compositional structure by decomposing temporally extended objectives into finite sequences of sub-goal transitions.
2. Foundation models demonstrate emergent semantic reasoning and code synthesis capabilities, suggesting they can automate expert-driven design processes in structured domains.
3. Natural language provides a universal medium for human intent, and its embedding into continuous semantic spaces may enable transferable skill representations across distinct tasks.
4. Sequential decision problems in complex environments possess inherent hierarchical sub-goal structure, where intermediate achievements can function as dense learning signals without distorting optimal behavior.
5. A persistent gap exists between high-level cognitive task descriptions and low-level control signals in embodied agents, motivating the need for grounded intermediate representations.
6. Formal properties of potential-based reward shaping indicate that structured intermediate incentives can preserve optimal policies while mitigating sample inefficiency in sparse reward settings.
7. Combinatorial and procedurally generated task distributions require reward specifications that support zero-shot composition of familiar sub-tasks into novel overall objectives.
