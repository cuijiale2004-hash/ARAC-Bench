Given Topic: Reinforcement learning fine-tuning for multimodal long-chain reasoning currently faces severe optimization barriers: reward sparsity on complex problems and vanishing advantages when instances are too easy or too difficult, which yields uninformative gradient signals and unstable training. Existing countermeasures—blind sample augmentation, selective data exclusion, or indirect reward densification—either distort the difficulty distribution, waste training data, or misalign intermediate supervision with final objectives. A fundamental gap is the lack of a mechanism to guarantee sufficient reward variance within response groups across dynamically shifting problem difficulties, ensuring each sample provides a meaningful optimization signal. This necessitates a principled approach to continuously adapt the effective difficulty of training instances to the model's evolving capability, maintaining balanced and discriminative feedback throughout optimization without discarding samples or introducing biased auxiliary objectives.

Given Inspiration:
1. The insight that perceived problem difficulty is not static but co-evolves with model capability; empirical rollout outcomes should continuously recalibrate the difficulty curriculum to sustain effective learning signals.
2. The perspective that semantically equivalent instances can serve as a controlled difficulty spectrum when visual and textual inputs are judiciously perturbed, enriching the reward landscape without altering core problem semantics.
3. The mathematical intuition that the strength of a normalized optimization signal is maximized when positive and negative outcomes are balanced, motivating active maintenance of such balance within each training batch.
4. The observation that narrowly comparing responses within a single problem and broadly comparing across related instances provide complementary signal characteristics; integrating both perspectives stabilizes gradient magnitudes.
5. The principle that correct solutions to harder instances deserve stronger positive reinforcement, while failures on easier instances should incur sharper negative feedback, relative to the group's average difficulty.
6. The engineering intuition that the observed spread of rewards within a group should modulate the confidence of advantage estimates, preventing minor reward fluctuations from being amplified into disproportionate optimization steps.
7. The motivation drawn from adaptive curriculum design that the training distribution must dynamically shift toward appropriate difficulty extremes to prevent convergence onto homogeneous, uninformative reward plateaus.
