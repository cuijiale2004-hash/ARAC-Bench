1. **Research Background and Existing Pain Points**

**Research Background:**
Recent advances in large multi-modal generative models have demonstrated impressive capabilities in multi-modal generation, including image and video generation. These models are typically built upon multi-step frameworks like diffusion and flow matching. While these paradigms have achieved state-of-the-art performance, they inherently limit their inference efficiency, requiring 40-100 Number of Function Evaluations (NFEs) to generate samples. For large-scale models, efficient sampling is paramount, as the continuous, long-term cost of inference often surpasses the one-time training cost, directly impacting their economic viability and practical deployment.

**Existing Pain Points:**
Existing few-step methods aimed at accelerating inference face a difficult trade-off between simplicity, efficiency, and quality:
(a) **Complexity and Instability:** Adversarial methods such as GANs and Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD variants) require auxiliary networks (e.g., discriminators) or frozen teacher models. This introduces training instability and sensitivity to hyperparameters, increases architectural complexity, and leads to high GPU memory overhead, hindering their scalability to large models (often resulting in Out-Of-Memory errors on ultra-large models).
(b) **Performance Degradation:** Methods that train from scratch without adversarial guidance, such as consistency models, often exhibit a sharp decline in quality at very low NFEs (< 4).
(c) **Dependency Constraints:** Prior 1-step/few-step methods depend on auxiliary trained models or frozen teacher models. For instance, GAN requires a trained discriminator, diffusion/consistency distillation requires a frozen teacher model, DMD requires training an auxiliary score function for fake data and a frozen teacher model, and DMD2 trains a GAN discriminator and a fake score function simultaneously. These dependencies limit simplicity and scalability in training large models.

2. **Core Research Motivation and Scientific Questions**

**Core Research Motivation:**
To address the challenges of existing few-step methods, the research aims to build a simple yet effective framework for training 1-step generative models that bypasses the need for fixed pretrained teacher models and avoids standard adversarial networks during training, making it ideal for building large-scale, efficient models. The motivation is to enhance one-step generation performance directly within the any-step flow matching framework without resorting to a GAN loss, which adds significant complexity, training instability, and memory overhead.

**Scientific Questions:**
How to create a self-contained, discriminator-free adversarial objective within the generative model itself to directly enhance one-step generation performance? How to recast the distribution matching problem between real and generated data into a practical velocity matching problem that straightens the generative trajectories, enabling few-step or 1-step generation without requiring external auxiliary networks or frozen teachers?

3. **Overall Core Idea and Design Philosophy**

**Overall Core Idea:**
The core idea is the introduction of twin trajectories featuring time-steps symmetric around $t = 0$. By extending the standard time interval from $t \in [0, 1]$ to $t \in [-1, 1]$, two trajectories originating from the noise distribution are conceptualized. The positive branch ($t > 0$) maps noise to real data, while the negative branch ($t < 0$) maps the same noise to "fake" data produced by the model itself, enabling simultaneous learning of both transformations. The learning objective is to minimize the discrepancy between the velocity fields of these two trajectories. This forces the model to learn a more robust and direct mapping from noise to data, thereby enhancing 1-step generation performance in a self-supervised manner.

**Design Philosophy:**
The design philosophy centers on simplicity and self-containment. By minimizing the difference between a "fake" and a "real" velocity field (which should ideally be zero), the method creates an internal self-adversarial signal, thus eliminating the need for an external GAN loss or discriminator. Furthermore, it integrates this self-adversarial and rectification objective with the standard base objective to train a single model that excels at both multi-step and few-step generation, avoiding the need for separate frozen teacher models for distillation.

4. **Core Innovation Points**

1.  **Twin Trajectory Concept for Self-Adversarial Training:** The introduction of a "twin trajectory" by extending the time interval from $[0, 1]$ to $[-1, 1]$. The positive branch maps noise to real data, and the negative branch maps noise to "fake" data generated by the model itself. This creates a self-contained, discriminator-free adversarial objective.
2.  **Rectification via Velocity Matching:** Recasting the distribution matching problem (minimizing KL divergence between fake and real distributions) into a practical velocity matching problem. By minimizing the discrepancy between the velocity fields of the twin trajectories, the method straightens the generative trajectories, enabling accurate approximation with large step sizes for 1-step generation.
3.  **Simple and Scalable Framework without Auxiliary Models:** TWINFLOW achieves 1-step generation without depending on auxiliary trained models (like GAN discriminators) or frozen teacher models, offering high simplicity and eliminating GPU memory cost associated with maintaining multiple model copies.
4.  **Unified Any-Step Formulation:** The integration of the self-adversarial loss (promoting multi-step generation fidelity) and the rectification loss (optimizing for few-step efficiency) into the broader any-step framework (RCGM), allowing a single model to be trained for both precise multi-step sampling and efficient few-step generation.
5.  **Practical Mixed Loss Implementation:** A practical training design that partitions each mini-batch into two subsets controlled by a balancing hyperparameter $\lambda$, accommodating the different requirements on the target time $r$ for the base objective and the TwinFlow objective within a single training step.

5. **Overview of the Overall Technical Solution**

The overall technical solution is built upon the any-step generation framework (RCGM). It introduces a novel training objective called $\mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}$ which comprises two main components: a self-adversarial loss and a rectification loss.
First, the standard time interval $[0, 1]$ is extended to $[-1, 1]$. A "fake" sample is generated by the model, and a corresponding "fake trajectory" is constructed where the network is trained with negative time inputs to learn the generative path to this fake data. This forms the self-adversarial loss $\mathcal{L}^{adv}$.
Second, to align the fake distribution with the real distribution, the method minimizes the KL divergence. By deriving the gradient of the KL divergence and utilizing the relationship between the score function and the velocity field under linear transport, the distribution matching problem is transformed into minimizing the difference between the velocity field of the real trajectory and the fake trajectory. A tractable rectification loss $\mathcal{L}^{rectify}$ is constructed using the stop-gradient operator to produce this gradient structure.
Finally, the overall loss combines the base objective $\mathcal{L}^{base}$ with $\mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}$. In implementation, mini-batches are partitioned to handle the differing target time requirements of the base and TwinFlow losses.

6. **Detailed Module Design**

**Module 1: Any-Step Generative Model Framework**
This module establishes the unified formulation covering multi-step and few-step paradigms. A prediction function is defined as $f(x_t, r) := x_r - x_t$, which predicts the target point $x_r$ from the current point $x_t$. The unified training objective is given by Equation (1). Multi-step models (diffusion/flow matching) are derived by setting $N=0$. Few-step models correspond to the $N=1$ case (recovering consistency models, shortcut models, or MeanFlow depending on specific time settings).

**Module 2: Twin Trajectory Construction and Self-Adversarial Training**
This module creates the self-adversarial objective. The standard learning process operates on $t \in [0, 1]$. To create the twin trajectory, the time interval is extended to $t \in [-1, 1]$. The negative half $t \in [-1, 0]$ is designated for learning a generative path from noise to "fake" data produced by the model itself.
A fake sample is generated: $x_{fake} = \hat{x}_t = x_t^{real} - F_\theta(x_t^{real}, r) \cdot t$ (using $t=1, r=0$ in implementation, so $x_{fake} = z - F_\theta(z, 0)$).
A "fake trajectory" is constructed where its perturbed version is defined as $x_{t'}^{fake} = \alpha(t') z_{fake} + \gamma(t') x_{fake}$, where $z_{fake} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and $t' \sim U(0, 1)$.
The network is trained with the flow matching objective on this trajectory using negative time inputs $-t' \in [-1, 0]$, yielding the self-adversarial loss $\mathcal{L}^{adv}(\theta)$ (Equation 2). Minimizing this loss teaches the network the negative time condition and the transformation from noise to fake data distribution.

**Module 3: Rectifying Real Trajectory via Velocity Matching**
This module minimizes the discrepancy between the twin trajectories. It treats the alignment of $p_{fake}$ and $p_{real}$ as a distribution matching problem by minimizing the KL divergence $D_{KL}(p_{fake} \| p_{real})$ (Equation 3).
Taking the gradient of the KL divergence (Equation 4) reveals it depends on the difference between the scores of the fake and real distributions. Using the relationship between score and velocity field under linear transport (Equation 5), the score difference is substituted with the velocity difference. This transforms the gradient of the KL divergence into an expectation over the inner product involving the velocity difference $\Delta v(x_t)$ (Equation 6 and 7).
To construct a tractable loss that produces this gradient structure, the Jacobian term is instantiated for the fake trajectory setup (Equation 8). The rectification loss $\mathcal{L}^{rectify}(\theta)$ (Equation 9) is then formulated using the stop-gradient operator $sg(\cdot)$. Minimizing this loss encourages the model to straighten the generative trajectories from noise to the data distribution.

**Module 4: Theoretical Transformation from Score to Velocity**
This module provides the derivation for the relationship between score and velocity used in Module 3.
Based on Equation 2 in Sun et al. (2025) with flow matching, the expression $f_x(F_\theta(x_t, t), x_t, t)$ is derived (Equation 11).
According to Theorem 2 in Sun et al. (2025), the same expression is equated to terms involving the score $s(x_t) = \nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t)$ (Equation 12).
By transposing the $s(x_t)$ term, the relationship between score and velocity is derived (Equation 13).

**Module 5: TwinFlow Objective with Practical Designs**
This module integrates the components. The method trains a single model for both multi-step and few-step generation by combining the self-adversarial loss and the rectification loss.
The final loss combines the base objective with the proposed terms: $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta) + (\mathcal{L}^{adv}(\theta) + \mathcal{L}^{rectify}(\theta)) = \mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta) + \mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}(\theta)$ (Equation 10).
Practically, since $\mathcal{L}^{base}$ requires $r$ sampled from $[0, 1]$ and $\mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}$ requires $r=0$, each mini-batch is partitioned into two subsets controlled by a hyperparameter $\lambda$. One portion computes $\mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}$ with $r=0$, while the remainder computes $\mathcal{L}^{base}$ with random $r \in [0, 1]$.

7. **All Mathematical Formulas and Symbol Definitions**

**Symbol Definitions:**
*   $D$: Dataset.
*   $p(x)$: Data distribution.
*   $p(x|c)$: Conditional distribution given a condition $c$.
*   $p(z)$: Source distribution (e.g., standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$).
*   $f(x_t, r)$: Prediction function defined as $x_r - x_t$.
*   $F_\theta$: Neural network.
*   $F_{\theta^-}$: No grad version of the neural network.
*   $x_t$: Perturbed sample.
*   $\alpha(t), \gamma(t)$: Linear transport coefficients.
*   $z, z_{fake}$: Noise variables ($\sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$).
*   $t, t'$: Time variables.
*   $s(x_t)$: Score function ($\nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t)$).
*   $\Delta v(x_t)$: Velocity difference ($v_{real}(x_t, t) - v_{fake}(x_t, -t)$).
*   $v_{real}, v_{fake}$: Velocity fields for real and fake trajectories.
*   $sg(\cdot)$: Stop-gradient operator.
*   $d(\cdot, \cdot)$: Metric function.
*   $\lambda$: Balancing hyperparameter for mini-batch partition.
*   $r$: Target time.

**Mathematical Formulas:**

(1) Unified training objective for any-step models:
$$ \mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x, z, \{t_i\}_{i=0}^{N+1}} \left[ d \left( \frac{dx_t}{dt}, \frac{1}{\Delta t} \left[ f_\theta(x_t, t_{N+1}) - \sum_{i=1}^N f_{\theta^-}(x_{t_i}, t_{i+1}) \right] \right) \right] $$
where $x_t = \alpha(t)z + \gamma(t)x$, $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, $t \sim U(0, T)$, $t_i \sim U(t_{i-1}, 0)$.

(2) Self-adversarial loss:
$$ \mathcal{L}^{adv}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x_{fake}, z_{fake}, t'} \left[ d \left( F_\theta(x_{t'}^{fake}, -t'), \frac{z_{fake} - x_{fake}}{} \right) \right] $$
(Note: Replicated exactly as provided in the source text).

(3) KL divergence between fake and real distributions:
$$ D_{KL}(p_{fake} \| p_{real}) = \mathbb{E}_{x_t, z, t} \left[ \log \left( \frac{p_{fake}(x_t)}{p_{real}(x_t)} \right) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{x_t, z, t} [ - (\log p_{real}(x_t) - \log p_{fake}(x_t)) ] $$

(4) Gradient of KL divergence:
$$ \nabla_\theta D_{KL}(p_{fake} \| p_{real}) = \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x_t, z, t} [\log p_{fake}(x_t) - \log p_{real}(x_t)] $$
$$ = \mathbb{E}_{x_t, z, t} \left[ \frac{\partial \log p_{fake}(x_t)}{\partial x_t} \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial \theta} - \frac{\partial \log p_{real}(x_t)}{\partial x_t} \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial \theta} \right] $$
$$ = \mathbb{E}_{x_t, z, t} \left[ \left( \underbrace{\nabla_{x_t} \log p_{fake}(x_t) - \nabla_{x_t} \log p_{real}(x_t)}_{s_{fake}(x_t) - s_{real}(x_t)} \right) \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial \theta} \right] $$

(5) Relationship between score and velocity field under linear transport ($\alpha(t)=t, \gamma(t)=1-t$):
$$ s(x_t) = \frac{-x_t + \gamma(t) \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t)}{\alpha(t)} = \frac{-x_t + (1 - t) \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t)}{t} $$

(6) KL gradient in terms of velocity:
$$ \nabla_\theta D_{KL} = \mathbb{E}_{x_t, z, t} \left[ \left( \frac{-x_t + (1 - t) \cdot F_\theta(x_t, -t)}{t} - \frac{(-x_t + (1 - t) \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t))}{t} \right) \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial \theta} \right] $$
$$ = \mathbb{E}_{x_t, z, t} \left[ - \frac{1 - t}{t} \cdot \left( \underbrace{F_\theta(x_t, -t)}_{v_{fake}(x_t, -t)} - \underbrace{F_\theta(x_t, t)}_{v_{real}(x_t, t)} \right) \frac{\partial x_t}{\partial \theta} \right] $$

(7) Velocity difference definition:
$$ \Delta v(x_t) := v_{real}(x_t, t) - v_{fake}(x_t, -t) $$

(8) Jacobian simplification:
$$ \frac{\partial x_{t'}^{fake}}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\partial (\alpha(t') z_{fake} + \gamma(t') x_{fake})}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\partial (\alpha(t') z_{fake} + \gamma(t') \hat{x}_t)}{\partial \theta} \propto - \left. \frac{\partial F_\theta(x_t^{real}, r)}{\partial \theta} \right|_{t=1, r=0} = - \frac{\partial F_\theta(z, 0)}{\partial \theta} $$

(9) Rectification loss:
$$ \mathcal{L}^{rectify}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x_t, x_{fake}, z_{fake}, t'} \left[ d \left( F_\theta(z, 0), sg \left[ \Delta v(x_{t'}^{fake}) + F_\theta(z, 0) \right] \right) \right] $$

(10) Overall loss:
$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta) + (\mathcal{L}^{adv}(\theta) + \mathcal{L}^{rectify}(\theta)) = \mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta) + \mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}(\theta) $$

(11) Derivation of $f_x$ (Appendix D):
$$ f_x(F_\theta(x_t, t), x_t, t) = \frac{\alpha(t) \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t) - \hat{\alpha}(t) \cdot x_t}{\alpha(t) \cdot \hat{\gamma}(t) - \hat{\alpha}(t) \cdot \gamma(t)} = \frac{t \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t) - x_t}{(t \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot (1 - t))} = \frac{t \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t) - x_t}{(-1)} = x_t - t \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t) $$

(12) Expression with score (Appendix D):
$$ f_x(F_\theta(x_t, t), x_t, t) = \frac{x_t + \alpha^2(t) \nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t)}{\gamma(t)} = \frac{x_t + t^2 \nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t)}{1 - t} = \frac{x_t + t^2 s(x_t)}{1 - t} $$

(13) Transformation from score to velocity (Appendix D):
$$ \frac{x_t + t^2 s(x_t)}{1 - t} = x_t - t \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t) $$
$$ \implies t^2 s(x_t) = (1 - t)(x_t - t \cdot F_\theta(x_t, t)) - x_t = -t x_t + (t^2 - t)F_\theta(x_t, t) $$
$$ \implies s(x_t) = \frac{-t x_t + (t^2 - t)F_\theta(x_t, t)}{t^2} = \frac{-x_t + (1 - t)F_\theta(x_t, t)}{t} $$

8. **Algorithm Pseudocode**

No explicit algorithm pseudocode block is provided in the original paper. The training process and implementation details are described textually in Section 3.3: The overall loss function combines the base objective with the proposed TwinFlow terms. In practical implementation of mixed loss, the $\mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta)$ and $\mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}(\theta)$ objectives impose different requirements on the target time $r$. $\mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta)$ requires $r$ to be sampled from $[0, 1]$, whereas $\mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}(\theta)$ necessitates a fixed target time of $r = 0$. To accommodate both within a single training step, each mini-batch is partitioned into two subsets. A balancing hyperparameter $\lambda$ controls the relative size of these subsets. One portion of the batch is used to compute $\mathcal{L}^{TwinFlow}(\theta)$ with $r = 0$, while the remainder is used for $\mathcal{L}^{base}(\theta)$ with a randomly sampled $r \in [0, 1]$.
