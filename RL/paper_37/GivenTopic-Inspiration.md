Given Topic: Multi-agent traffic simulation constitutes a foundational capability for verifying autonomous driving safety. Current data-driven approaches predominantly rely on maximum-likelihood estimation and behavior cloning, which suffer from pronounced distributional shifts when open-loop training objectives are applied to closed-loop sequential prediction. Autoregressive behavior generation paradigms are particularly susceptible to compounding errors during long-horizon rollouts, degrading physical plausibility. Furthermore, standard supervised losses are inherently misaligned with downstream evaluation criteria—such as safety-critical outcomes—that are scalar, sparse, and non-differentiable, rendering them inaccessible to direct gradient optimization. The core problem is to align simulated multi-agent behaviors with high-level human preferences and task-specific evaluation metrics while preserving the naturalistic distribution of real-world driving and mitigating error accumulation in closed-loop simulation.

Given Inspiration:
1. The structural gap between imitative learning objectives and downstream evaluation metrics in sequential decision systems, where critical safety outcomes are non-differentiable and thus inaccessible to standard gradient-based supervision.
2. Post-training alignment paradigms in generative modeling, where policies are refined via reinforcement signals to satisfy high-level preferences rather than merely maximizing data likelihood.
3. The covariate shift dilemma in closed-loop autoregressive generation, wherein training-time conditioning on ground-truth histories diverges from test-time autoregressive rollout, causing progressive degradation of trajectory fidelity.
4. The tension between metric-driven optimization and distributional fidelity during policy refinement, motivated by the tendency of reward maximization to overwrite pre-trained behavioral priors.
5. The relatively constrained and predictable structure of traffic-domain reward landscapes compared to open-ended reasoning tasks, suggesting that explicit threshold-based reward normalization can provide more stable policy gradients than sample-relative baselines.
