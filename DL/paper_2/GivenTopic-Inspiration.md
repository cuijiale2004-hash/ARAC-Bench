Given Topic: The theoretical foundations of graph representation learning remain incomplete regarding the statistical limits of sample complexity. While classical deep learning theory provides generalization guarantees under independent sampling assumptions, graph-structured data inherently violates this premise because observations are coupled through relational edges and iterative neighborhood aggregation. Existing theoretical analyses predominantly focus on the inductive graph-level regime, leaving the transductive node-level setting—which is prevalent in practical applications—largely unexplored from a minimax perspective. The core problem is to characterize the fundamental lower bounds on generalization error and to determine how topological properties, such as connectivity patterns and mixing behavior, shape the effective sample size required for reliable learning on structured data.

Given Inspiration:
1. Classical minimax theory for deep networks establishes risk scaling under independent samples, but graph-structured observations violate independence through relational coupling and neighborhood aggregation.
2. The transductive node-level setting on a single fixed graph introduces strong spatial correlations among labels, creating a statistical regime fundamentally distinct from the inductive graph-level case.
3. Spectral graph theory and mixing time analysis suggest that a graph's expansion properties and bottleneck structures govern how far information can propagate through local aggregation operations.
4. The effective sample size in networked data may collapse when receptive fields overlap extensively, indicating that raw label counts overestimate the true information available for learning.
5. Community structures with sparse inter-cluster connectivity create information bottlenecks that limit the diversity of supervised signals accessible to any estimator.
6. Empirical evidence suggests that many real-world benchmarks exhibit slow mixing and strong intra-community correlations, implying that practical learning performance may be constrained by topology rather than model capacity alone.
